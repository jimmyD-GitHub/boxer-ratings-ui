Boxing fans are all too familiar with the word controversy, defined as something causing "prolonged public disagreement or heated discussion". This article examines 5 bouts from the recent history of the sport that ended in highly controversial fashion. The absence from this list of fights such as Mike Tyson vs Evander Holyfield II or Roberto Duran vs Sugar Ray Leonard II may seem like glaring omissions to some. However, the circumstances surrounding those fights were more "shocking" than they were controversial.

No reasonable person could claim that referee Mills Lane was wrong to disqualify Tyson during "The Bite Fight" in 1997. Similarly, no one argued against Wayne Kelly or Eddie Cotton's disqualifications of Polish Heavyweight Andrew Golota for the low blows he inflicted on Riddick Bowe. Without question though, the manner in which the 5 contests detailed below ended led to passionate debate amongst fans, boxers and journalists alike.

5

Roy Jones Jr

Roy Jones Jr vs Montell Griffin I

Taj Majal Hotel & Casino, Atlantic City - 21st March 1997

Olympic silver medallist Roy Jones Jr (then 34-0 ) was boxing at 175lbs for the 2nd time as a professional when Montell "Ice" Griffin challenged him for the WBC Light Heavyweight title in March 1997. Griffin (then 26-0 ) entered the ring having defeated former Jones adversary James "Lights Out" Toney in a rematch just 3 months previously, but few in attendance expected the Chicago fighter to maintain his unbeaten record.

Some bookmakers favoured Jones by odds as high as 17-1 when the contest was first announced. Widely regarded as the most accomplished boxer in the sport, Jones had already captured world titles at Middleweight and Super Middleweight, before becoming "interim" WBC champion with a unanimous decision victory over Mike McCallum on his Light Heavyweight debut. Jones boasted a significant speed and skill advantage over most opponents and he also enjoyed the luxury of a 4 inch height and reach advantage over the short but powerful Griffin.

For the first 6 rounds of the fight the challenger made a mockery of the long odds against him. Jones was continually falling short with a tentative left jab thrown from long range, as if hoping to lure his opponent into a badly judged counterattack. The ploy did little to trouble Griffin, who was frequently able to trap Jones in corners of the ring. Whenever the champions back touched the ropes Griffin threw a combination to the body and finished it with a sweeping left hook to the head. Commentating for HBO, veteran boxing analyst Larry Merchant described the bout as "...by far the most competitive fight of Roy Jones Jr's professional career.".

In the 5th round Jones finally began to assert himself on proceedings, finding the target with some well-timed right-hand leads. Griffin was still moving forward and keeping Jones under pressure, but the champion was more prepared to hold the centre of the ring and commit to his punches. With the fight becoming more combative and less tactical, Jones made a breakthrough in the 7th round, flooring Griffin with a short left hook while the challenger was momentarily caught off balance. Griffin rose quickly and questioned the legitimacy of the knock down but it appeared that the momentum of the bout had shifted toward Jones.

The pivotal moment of the contest arrived in round 9. During a frenzied exchange of punches Jones hurt Griffin with a right cross that connected high on the side of the challenger's head. Jones followed up with a sustained attack, pursuing his opponent around the ring as Griffin attempted to recover his senses. With his back to the ropes and Jones bearing down on him Griffin chose to drop to one knee in an effort to buy more time. As he did so Jones hesitated, but then struck his downed opponent with a right hand and a left hook, both of which landed solidly to the head. Griffin initially looked at referee Tony Perez as if too complain but then slumped forward, apparently unconscious.

"I thought he was going to come back up. That's why I hit him. You don't have time to stop and think whether he's going to come back up."

Roy Jones Jr speaking to The New York Times in 1997

Tony Perez counted Griffin out as he lay prone on the canvas and Jones turned away with his hands raised in celebration. Amid the pandemonium that followed, New Jersey boxing commissioner Larry Hazard entered the ring and conducted a brief conversation with Perez. When the result of the fight was announced a few minutes later it transpired that Roy Jones Jr had lost his unbeaten record by way of disqualification. Montell Griffin was the new WBC Light Heavyweight champion of the world.

Jones had clearly committed a blatant foul but many observers questioned the circumstances of his disqualification. Perez stated that he had been prepared to penalise Jones with a point deduction if Griffin had been able to carry on. However, it was judged that Griffin was in no position to continue. Griffin was lucid and composed when interviewed in the ring immediately after the fight and did not appear to be concussed or injured.

While hardly insignificant, the punches Jones landed on Griffin seemed less full blooded than the illegal blows Nigel Been had been guilty of during his WBO Middleweight title fight with Iran Barkley in 1990. The fouls Jones committed were more reminiscent of the punches Tim Witherspoon landed after knocking down Frank Bruno during a WBA Heavyweight title defence in 1986. Witherspoon was not penalised by referee Isidro Rodriguez on that occasion and Benn only received a warning from Carlos Padilla.

The new champion acknowledged the unsatisfactory nature of his victory ahead of the rematch later that year. "Don't even call me the Light Heavyweight champion", he told Michael Hirsley of the Chicago Tribune , "After I beat him Thursday night, I will welcome being called the champion. Right now, I don't want to hear that". As it was, Griffin had the misfortune to encounter Jones at his absolute best when the pair met again in Connecticut on August 7th 1997. A fired up Jones settled any doubts about his supremacy with a decisive 1st round KO win.

4

Roberto Duran

Ken Buchanon vs Roberto Duran

Madison Square Garden, New York - 26th June 1972

Scotland's Ken Buchanan was making the 3rd defence of his WBA World Lightweight title when he faced 21 year old Roberto Duran (then 28-0 ) of Panama in June 1972. The ferocious young contender had ended all but 4 of his previous bouts inside the distance but Buchanan (then 43-1 ) seemed unperturbed at the weigh-in on the day of the fight.

"Manos de Piedra" was boxing in the USA for just the 2nd time in his career and was still something of an unknown quantity to New York fight fans. Buchanan, a classy orthodox boxer with a beautiful left jab, was making his 3rd of 5 appearances at Madison Square Garden. The champion had twice overcome another Panamanian, Ismael Laguna, to earn his title and was considered a healthy favourite to retain it.

In many ways the opening ten seconds of the 1st round served as a microcosm of the entire fight. Buchanan circled to his left and shot out one of his trademark jabs as the challenger rushed toward him. Duran slipped the punch with a subtle movement of his head and countered with a right hand over the top of Buchanan's left arm that dropped the champion for a mandatory 8 count. Buchanan rose quickly and fought back well for the remainder of the session but the die had been cast.

The action followed a similar pattern throughout the contest. Buchanan, a master at controlling his opponents from long range behind a perpetual torrent of searing jabs, was never able to exert authority over the rampaging challenger. Duran stalked the champion at a relentless pace, using clever head movement and a debilitating body attack to negate Buchanan's strengths. The British boxer was being countered effectively from distance and over powered when Duran forced him to fight at close quarters.

Round 5 was a particularly good one for Duran. The Panamanian manoeuvred Buchanan into a corner and scored freely with a series of powerful right crosses. The champion did well to survive the round but returned to his corner with a cut under his left eye. The fight had become a demoralizing one for Buchanan. Even though he enjoyed sporadic success, on the occasions when he was able to land cleanly Duran took his punches without effect. For long periods of the bout Buchanan was trapped on the ropes, where Duran constantly bore into him with his head and shoulders.

By the time the 13th round began any hope that Buchanan could salvage something from the fight appeared futile. It was difficult to argue that he'd won any more than 2 of the preceding 12 rounds and he looked to be physically spent. The 13th round was another strong one for Duran but in the final seconds Buchanan stood his ground and threw a defiant burst of punches. Referee Johnny LoBianco stepped forward to intervene as the pair continued to trade blows after the bell. As he did so Duran got the last shot in with a right uppercut that connected squarely with the champion's groin.

"He hit me in between the legs. I ended up in hospital for 10 says because I was urinating blood...people didn't realise what I went through."

Ken Buchanan speaking to Craig Birch in 2017

Buchanon immediately tumbled to the canvas and rolled over in agony as his seconds attempted to help him back to the corner. LoBianco observed Buchanan closely as he sat on his stool receiving treatment. A conversation between the two ensued during which Buchanan appeared to protest vigorously, but a few seconds later LoBianco signalled the end of the fight. Roberto Duran was announced as the new WBA Lightweight champion of the world, by way of technical knockout.

Buchanan may have been hopelessly behind in the fight, but any time a world title changes hands on such a flagrant foul the result leaves a bitter taste in the mouth. Interviewed by ABC commentator Don Dunphy in the ring immediately after the fight, LoBianco stated that "...he got hit just after the bell rang, I didn't think it should be counted...he appeared to get hit in the lower part of his abdomen, it wasn't a low blow..."

Plainly speaking, LoBianco has contrived to miss an obvious infraction carried out right under his nose. To add insult to injury, the referee then proceeded to award victory to the offender, despite conceding that the decisive punch had been landed after the bell. Buchanan should have been allowed 5 minutes to recover from the low blow. Failing that, surely he should have been given the opportunity to finish the fight.

Duran has always denied hitting Buchanan low and claims that the final punch was to the rib cage. However, in his 2016 autobiography I Am Duran the legendary fighter accepted that his New York connections may have proved beneficial: "What is true is that if Ray Arcel and Freddie Brown hadn't been in my corner, I wouldn't have become world champion...they knew a number of the judges and the referee for that fight."

Duran had produced a wonderful performance to claim his first world title and a glittering career lay ahead of him. However, this would not be the last time one of his Madison Square Garden triumphs became marred by controversy. Former amateur stand out Davey Moore insisted that "The Hands Of Stone" had thumbed in the eye during their WBA Light Middleweight world title fight in 1983. Duran captured the title with an 8th round TKO.

3

Carl Froch

Carl Froch vs George Groves I

Phones 4u Arena, Manchester, England - 23rd November 2013

Carl "The Cobra" Froch was nearing the end of a superb career as he prepared to meet domestic rival "Saint George" Groves in November 2013. A meeting between the two British fighters had seemed unlikely, with Froch apparently in negotiations to defend his IBF and WBA Super Middleweight titles against WBC champion Andre Ward. Froch (then 31-2 ) had unified the titles with a unanimous decision rematch victory over Mikkel Kessler earlier that year and seemed keen to gain revenge over Ward, the only other man to defeat him in a professional ring.

The Ward fight failed to materialise and Groves (then 19-0 ) became mandatory challenger for Froch's IBF world title. The London fighter had boxed on the undercard of Froch vs Kessler II and caught the eye with a 5th round KO of Noe Gonzalez Alcoba. Groves irked Froch considerably in the build up to that event by insisting that he was capable of taking the Nottingham man's titles.

Froch was listed as a clear odds-on favourite at 2-7 with most bookmakers and didn't appear to be taking his challenger seriously in the build up to the fight. As he admitted to broadcaster Darren Fletcher in 2019: "I thought I was going to go in and walk through him...as soon as I land this 10 ounce glove on his chin, it's lights out..."

Groves seemed tremendously confident despite his underdog status and lack of world level experience. An ill-timed split with trainer Adam Booth shortly before the fight did little to faze him. Groves even had the temerity to warn the champion about the kind of punches he intended to land in the 1st round when the boxers came face to face at a pre-fight press conference.

The challenger was as good as his word. Groves commanded the centre of the ring from the opening bell and was quick to gain the ascendancy as the two fighters traded jabs. Froch appeared tentative and Groves took advantage, scoring with right hands after pushing the champion back to the ropes. Froch attempted a counter attack after being backed up for the third time in the round, only to walk on to a huge Groves overhand right that left him flat on his back. It was a heavy knock down.

The champion rose on unsteady legs and stumbled back into the ropes. Few fighters could have beaten the count after absorbing the full force of such a powerful blow. Fewer still would have been able to survive the follow up attacks Groves launched in a bid to end the bout early. Whatever your opinion of Carl Froch, it is debateable whether Britain has ever produced a tougher or more courageous boxer.

Groves had momentum in the fight and was in no mood to relinquish it. The action fell into a noticeable pattern. Groves held the centre of the ring as Froch circled him and jabbed from range. Time and time again the challenger would exploit Froch's low left glove with a measured right to the head. Froch pawed with his left hand while Groves jabbed with speed and accuracy.

The champion was losing the rounds but slowly began to inch his way back into the contest. When Froch was able to engage Groves at close range his superior physical strength became evident. Groves threw punches in ones and two's but Froch was the fighter looking to fire off combinations.

Froch was forced to display his much vaunted durability for long periods of the bout but finally began to make an impression on Groves in rounds 7 and 8. For the first time in the fight Groves came under pressure and started to retreat. Froch was timing his shots better and seemed to have found a rhythm.

The key moment of the contest arrived in round 9. During a thrilling exchange of punches Froch wobbled Groves with a sweeping right hand that glanced off the top of the challenger's head. Groves held on to Froch's arms and appeared composed as he protected himself. Froch followed up with another strong barrage and found the target with a straight right which knocked Groves back into the ropes. As Froch advanced Groves came forward to meet him and again looked to hold. As he did so he stumbled forward and was intercepted by referee Howard Foster, who shielded Groves with one arm and waved the other to signal the end of the fight.

"George had his head low and I had a free shot and the referee had a split-second decision to make. It was dangerous and he had to put the safety of the fighter first."

Carl Froch speaking to BBC Sport in 2013

As far as underwhelming endings to great fights are concerned, it is difficult to think of one more disappointing than this. Howard Foster had the welfare of George Groves firmly at the forefront of his mind, but the soft stoppage was greeted with widespread incredulity from those in attendance and boxing fans watching at home.

The sense of frustration was exacerbated by a feeling that Foster had applied double standards in his interpretation of events. If the bar for intervening on a boxer's behalf was set so low in the referee's mind, surely he should have waved the fight off after Froch was decked in the opening round? Indeed, the champion had seemingly taken worse punishment than Groves had suffered in round 9 on numerous occasions prior to that, particularly in rounds 5 and 6.

Groves himself certainly felt that the referee had entered the ring with pre-conceived notions in his head about the challenger's capacity to absorb punishment. As he explained to boxingscene.com the day after the fight: "There's no way it should have been stopped. It's a bitter pill to swallow. I think I'm a victim of my 'chinny' reputation and a victim of Froch's supposed punching power."

The contention generated by what was generally considered to be an unfair refereeing decision led to a highly anticipated rematch the following year. On that occasion there was to be no quarrel about the outcome. A more focused and better prepared Carl Froch concluded his distinguished career with an 8th round TKO in front of 80,000 paying spectators at Wembley Stadium in London.

2

Julio Cesar Chavez

Julio Cesar Chavez vs Meldrick Taylor I

Hilton Hotel, Las Vegas - 17th March 1990

There was great excitement among boxing fans when Mexican superstar Julio Cesar Chavez and US Olympic gold medallist Meldrick Taylor stepped into the ring for an eagerly awaited Light Welterweight unification bout in March 1990. As well as his WBC title, Chavez was hoping to preserve an incredible 68-0 professional record that also featured world championship victories at Lightweight and Super Featherweight. Taylor ( then 24-0-1 ) was making his 3rd defence of the IBF Light Welterweight title he had earned with a 12th round TKO win over James "Buddy" McGirt in September 1988.

The event was memorably billed as "Thunder And Lightning" in reference to the contrast in fighting styles between the two participants. Chavez, a methodical pressure fighter in the classic Mexican tradition, was judged a narrow favourite at 8-5 when betting opened on the contest. Taylor was widely expected to cause Chavez considerable difficulty with his blinding hand speed and clever lateral movement.

Chavez was known as a slow starter and it came as no great surprise when Taylor out worked his opponent in the opening two rounds. However, by the time the bell sounded to end round 5 it was clear that the Mexican's unbeaten record was in serious jeopardy. Taylor's hugely experienced trainer Lou Duva placed great emphasis on keeping the Philadelphian away from the ropes during preparation for the bout and his fighter was executing their game plan to perfection.

Chavez held the centre of the ring and looked to probe and press but Taylor simply refused to be pushed back. Whenever it seemed like the Mexican might begin to build momentum Taylor would move to the side and turn his man. Every time Chavez threw a combination Taylor would respond with a cleaner, faster burst of punches and for the most part it was Taylor who both initiated and finished the exchanges. Chavez's acclaimed body attack had been largely nullified and it was his opponent who surprised onlookers by getting the best of the action on the inside.

In rounds 7, 8 and 9 Taylor felt emboldened to stand toe to toe with Chavez. In the first half of the fight the younger man had alternated between smooth movement and rapid punching but now he refused to compromise. Throughout his career Taylor had been prepared to absorb punishment from opponents who would have enjoyed far less success had he not felt compelled to engage them in fierce infighting. This occasion was to be no different. Taylor continued to win rounds with his tremendous work rate and lightning hand speed, but his face began to swell and bleed as a result of his crowd pleasing instincts.

"Taylor was brilliant, too slick, too fast, and too smart. He won rounds, he controlled Chavez. It was impossible to take your eyes off it. Chavez was simply relentless and he still hurt and marked Taylor in rounds that he lost."

Steve Bunce writing for The Independent in 2020

Chavez had thrown less than half of the punches that had come his way and had connected with a far lower percentage than he normally would, but the great champion's resolve never wavered. In the 10th session Taylor started to show signs of fatigue from his incredible effort. Chavez scored more regularly with straight right hands to the head and his celebrated left hooks became a factor. Taylor's legs appeared to be weakening. For the first time in the fight a strong case could be made that Chavez had won a round.

The action followed a similar pattern during the 11th, but when the bell sounded to end the round even Chavez's most dedicated supporters must have felt that their hero's resurgence had come too late to save him from defeat. They were right to be concerned. Judge Jerry Roth had Taylor ahead by 7 points on his scorecard and Dave Moretti also had Chavez trailing by 5 points. Amazingly, judge Chuck Giampa scored the fight for the Mexican by a single point after 11 completed rounds.

Chavez needed a knockout to retain his title but as the 12th round progressed he did not appear to have enough energy to get the job done. Despite his dominant display, Taylor looked much the worse for wear, but there seemed little danger that he would not go the distance. In the final 60 seconds of the contest the intensity of the fighting suddenly ramped up as both men looked to finish strongly. Chavez stunned Taylor with a left hook, right cross combination that elicited a determined response from his opponent. Taylor pursued the Mexican into a corner, where Chavez was able to spin away from danger before flooring his adversary with a beautifully timed straight right to the chin.

Taylor's back hit the canvas with just 15 seconds remaining on the clock. Although badly shaken, he was able to climb to his feet as referee Richard Steele reached a count of 6. Steele completed the mandatory 8 count then peered closely at the fighter to assess his condition. Steele asked Taylor if he was alright ("Are you alright? You OK?"). When Taylor appeared distracted and did not immediately respond Steele waved his arms to signal the end of the fight. Julio Cesar Chavez had become the unified WBC/IBF Light Welterweight champion of the world. The official announcement recorded a technical knockout at 2:58 of the 12th and final round. It was the most dramatic and shocking end to a world title bout in the entire history of the sport.

"He hit me with a good punch, but I got right up. I knew I was ahead. I nodded I was OK but he waved it off."

Meldrick Taylor speaking to Ken Hissner in 2009

Taylor and his seconds were infuriated by the referee's decision but Steele defended his actions when questioned by Larry Merchant in the ring immediately after the fight. "I'm not the timekeeper and I don't care about the time", he explained, "...when I see a man who has had enough, I'm stopping the fight...There is no fight worth a man's life."

In many ways, Steele's position was entirely justifiable. Taylor had produced a text book example of how not to act when rising from a knock down during a prize fight. He hadn't responded verbally to Steele's questions. He hadn't raised his hands or bounced on the spot to demonstrate his ability to continue. He hadn't looked at the referee. Worse still, he had looked away from Steele while he was being spoken too. It was only after the referee waved the fight off that Taylor became responsive and animated.

That being said, surely Steele could have made allowances based on the small amount of time left in the round. The Nevada State Athletic Commission rules stated that the 12th and final round was the only round of the contest in which a boxer could be saved by the bell. If that rule didn't come into play under these circumstances, then what was the point in having it at all?

Steele was correct in asserting that the business of monitoring time was not his responsibility. However, he had to have been aware that only seconds remained. Flashing red lights had been installed above the ring posts that became active for the final 10 seconds of each round. As Steele looked into Taylor's eyes he couldn't have failed to realise the significance of a large flashing red light situated a few inches behind the fighter's head.

And hadn't Steele been somewhat hasty in making his decision? No more than 2 seconds elapsed between the referee completing his mandatory 8 count and the point at which he waved his arms in the air. Couldn't he have moved a few steps back and beckoned Taylor forward? Referees have performed that simple test of a boxers balance countless times over the years, but Steele seemed awfully keen to terminate proceedings as quickly as possible.

Taylor's management team called a press conference the following day at which Lou Duva laid the blame for Taylor's defeat squarely at Richard Steele's door. "It's a hell of a way to lose a fight." he told the Los Angeles Times , "We were winning the fight for 11 rounds, two minutes and 58 seconds. Then the referee took it away from us".

Duva's protestations were understandable but it should be noted that his own conduct during those fateful moments has also been questioned. Many journalists and spectators present at the fight have claimed that the reason Taylor did not respond to Steele's questions was because he was distracted by Duva , who had apparently jumped up onto the ring apron in something of a panic on seeing his charge hit the floor.

And then there was the not insignificant matter of the instructions Taylor had received from his cornermen prior to the final round. "You need this round..." his co-trainer George Benton has informed him. "The fight is hanging on this round..." Lou Duva had insisted. It may have been that the two veteran boxing men were untrusting of Las Vegas judges, with good reason, given Chuck Giampa's ludicrous scoring of the bout. However, surely in the context of this fight the best advice they could have offered their man was to stay out of trouble and to avoid taking undue risks. As it was, Taylor had been desperate to attack Chavez in the final seconds of the battle, seemingly unaware that the war had already been won.

1

Sergey Kovalev

Andre Ward vs Sergey Kovalev II

Mandalay Bay Hotel & Casino, Events Center, Las Vegas - 17th June 2017

Andre Ward had become the first American Olympic boxing champion since 1996 when he captured Light Heavyweight gold at the Athens games of 2004. Thirteen years later he was hoping to round off an equally impressive unbeaten professional career by defending his WBA and IBF Light Heavyweight world titles in a rematch with former champion Sergey "Krusher" Kovalev.

The Russian (then 30-1-1 ) was desperate to regain the titles he had lost to Ward 7 months earlier. Many observers felt that Kovalev had done enough to earn a decision over Ward on that occasion but all three judges scored the bout in favour of the challenger by tallies of 114-113. Kovalev, a noted puncher, had dropped Ward heavily in the 2nd round with a well-executed one-two.

Both men were regarded as being, pound for pound, amongst the top 10 boxers in the world at the time they faced each other. Ward had become a two division world champion with victory in their first fight and was largely considered to be one of the best Super Middleweights of all time, having unified the WBA and WBC titles at 168lbs. Kovalev, himself a celebrated amateur , had ended 26 of his 31 contests inside the distance before suffering the first defeat of his professional career to Ward.

Ward began the contest cautiously. In the opening 3 rounds he stayed on the outside and used his speed and footwork to avoid any serious conflict with Kovalev. The Russian pressed forward and seemed eager to assert his authority with long probing jabs. Ward was choosing his moments to come forward carefully. When he did so the champion would often grapple with Kovalev, as if intent on sapping the Russian's strength using rough, physical tactics.

In the final minute of the 2nd round Kovalev doubled over in pain after a left hook from Ward strayed low. The punch appeared to land just on or below the waist line of the challenger's trunks but referee Tony Weeks was quick to signal a time out. It was the first of many instances of alleged fouls by Ward that Kovalev would draw attention to during the contest.

Ward was causing Kovalev some discomfort, but it was the former champion who produced the sharper, more eye-catching work. Both fighters struggled to land cleanly, but the Russian stole the early rounds with accurate jabs and the occasional stinging right hand. Towards the end of the 4th round Weeks again called time out to warn both boxers about their conduct, after another period of wrestling at close quarters. The bout was turning into a dirty, ill-tempered affair.

"He hit me low already many times in the fight...I don't have metal balls."

Sergey Kovalev speaking at the post fight press conference in 2017

From the 4th round onwards Ward began to have more success with his own jab and this allowed the American to position himself for some excellent right crosses and left hooks. The action followed a similar pattern in rounds 5 and 6. The champion had closed the gap on the scorecards and the contest balanced on a knife edge.

The 7th round was a far tighter session. With 45 seconds remaining Kovalev folded over in pain for the 2nd time in the bout after another low left hand from Ward. This time Weeks was less sympathetic. The referee dismissed Kovalev's complaint and instructed the two fighters to continue boxing. Television replays indicated that the punch had been no less borderline than the foul Weeks had called a time out for in the 2nd round.

The fight had been engrossing rather than dramatic but everything changed in the 8th round. For the first time both men held their ground and began to exchange punches in the centre of the ring. With more than 90 seconds still remaining in the round Ward bullied Kovalev to the ropes and hurt the Russian with uppercuts to the body. Kovalev went on the defensive and complained to Weeks that the punches had been low. Sensing weakness, Ward kept the challenger under pressure and exploited a lazy left jab to rock Kovalev again with a tremendous counter right cross.

This time Kovalev was badly hurt. Ward followed up with a vicious barrage of hooks to the body and head as his floundering opponent tried to hold and stall for time. Kovalev found himself trapped in a corner of the ring on unsteady legs, but still continued to defend himself with his back to the ropes. Weeks broke the fighters apart but as soon as he moved away Ward continued his assault. The champion connected with three consecutive left hands to Kovalev's waist line that appeared to get progressively lower with each impact.

Kovalev doubled over in pain for the 3rd time in the bout then sat back onto one of the ropes. Weeks again rushed in to separate the fighters, presumably with the intention of calling a time out for low blows, or failing that, to administer a count. Somewhat inexplicably, the referee instead waved his arms to indicate an end to the fight. Andre Ward had retained his Light Heavyweight titles with the final victory of an unbeaten professional career, by way of technical knockout.

"I thought the stoppage was terrible. Guys get hurt in fights. It doesn't mean you stop a fight every time somebody gets hurt."

Paulie Malignaggi speaking to Sky Sports in 2017

Weeks' handling of the contest left the challenger's team incandescent. Kovalev's manager Kathy Duva was even more scathing about the referee's performance than her father-in-law Lou Duva had been about Richard Steele 27 years earlier. "It's a disgrace..." she informed journalists after the fight, "The only fair verdict is for this to be overturned and declared a no contest."

A perception exists among many of Ward's supporters that Kovalev had been looking for a way out of the fight, and that the Russian was attempting to deflect attention from his own failings by crying foul. "He was ready to quit. Point blank, period. I don’t care what nobody say..." former WBC Lightweight champion Adrien Broner told boxingscene.com , "I’ve been hit low before. And I’m not saying he got hit low. But even if he did, it didn’t make him quit."

The argument that Kovalev had been "trying to quit" is interesting and deserves careful scrutiny. Had Kovalev been "trying to quit" when he doubled over from low blows in the 2nd round? That would be a strange state of affairs, given that he had won the early rounds and came into the rematch confident that he had been cheated out of victory in his first fight with Ward. Tony Weeks saw fit to offer Kovalev time to recover after that initial low blow, but oddly determined that all subsequent low blows landed in the same area were legitimate.

Heading into the 8th round two of the judges, Glen Feldman and Dave Moretti, had Kovalev one point down on their scorecards. Judge Steve Weisfeld had the challenger 3 points ahead. This was a tough, competitive world title fight in which neither man had taken severe punishment. Certainly not the kind of bout where you expect the referee to "save" one of the boxers at the first sign of trouble.

Kovalev had undoubtedly found himself in dire straits during the 8th round and a stoppage victory for Ward looked imminent. However, when a boxer finds himself in such a perilous position he should be able to depend on the referee, then more than ever, to uphold the laws of the game. If the Russian had been allowed 5 minutes to recover from any of the three low blows that ended the contest the implications could have been huge. A 5 minute break might have enabled Kovalev to shake off the effects of the Ward right cross which effectively ended the fight.
